# Research Protocol: Cognitive Biases in Decision Making

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on cognitive biases as structured deviations from normative or criterion-based judgment. It is intended for decision-making experiments, behavioral-economics studies, risk-perception studies, calibration research, debiasing interventions, human factors research, institutional review systems, and human-AI decision-support evaluation.

## Core constructs

### Bias type

Bias type identifies the dominant pattern under study, such as confirmation bias, anchoring, framing, overconfidence, hindsight bias, base-rate neglect, availability bias, loss aversion, status quo bias, omission bias, or bias blind spot.

### Frame

Frame records whether equivalent information is presented as gain, loss, survival, mortality, risk reduction, harm avoidance, certainty, uncertainty, success, or failure.

### Gain/loss domain

Gain/loss domain identifies whether the decision is framed around gains, losses, mixed prospects, or neutral outcomes.

### Anchor value

Anchor value captures initial numerical or contextual starting points that may pull subsequent estimates.

### Base rate

Base rate records prior probability or population-level frequency.

### Representativeness

Representativeness measures similarity to a category, prototype, stereotype, or vivid case.

### Evidence valence

Evidence valence records whether evidence supports, contradicts, or is neutral toward the participant's prior belief.

### Confirmation congruence

Confirmation congruence measures whether selected evidence matches existing belief or expectation.

### Confidence rating

Confidence rating measures subjective certainty.

### Actual accuracy

Actual accuracy records observed correctness or criterion agreement.

### Calibration error

Calibration error measures the gap between subjective confidence and actual accuracy.

### Overconfidence

Overconfidence occurs when confidence exceeds accuracy.

### Loss-aversion parameter

Loss aversion measures asymmetric weighting of losses relative to gains.

### Probability weighting

Probability weighting measures subjective distortion of objective probabilities.

### Risky choice

Risky choice records whether the participant chose a risky option over a certain or safer option.

### Debiasing condition

Debiasing condition records training, checklist, accountability, base-rate prompt, premortem, consider-the-opposite, statistical feedback, or no intervention.

### Institutional review flag

Institutional review flag records whether a decision should receive second review due to high stakes, uncertainty, low confidence, disagreement, or vulnerable populations.

## Recommended study designs

1. Framing experiment using equivalent gain/loss descriptions.
2. Anchoring experiment with arbitrary and informative anchors.
3. Confirmation-bias experiment using evidence-search or belief-updating tasks.
4. Overconfidence and calibration study using confidence judgments.
5. Prospect-theory task estimating loss aversion and probability weighting.
6. Base-rate neglect task using representativeness manipulations.
7. Hindsight-bias experiment using pre-outcome and post-outcome predictability judgments.
8. Debiasing intervention study with pre/post measurement.
9. Group decision study comparing individual judgment, independent review, and deliberation.
10. Human-AI decision-support study measuring automation bias, verification burden, and calibrated trust.

## Measurement cautions

- Distinguish systematic bias from random error.
- Do not treat all deviations from a formal model as irrational without examining goals, context, environment, and information access.
- Measure base rates, cue validity, and environmental structure.
- Separate confidence from accuracy.
- Separate affective response from probability judgment where possible.
- Include response time, cognitive load, and time pressure.
- Model participant and item effects because bias expression varies across people and tasks.
- Avoid blaming individuals for institutionally induced bias.
- Include fairness and contestability when bias affects access, care, rights, employment, finance, or safety.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Cognitive biases affect institutional decisions in hiring, lending, medicine, policing, law, insurance, education, public benefits, and algorithmic systems. Bias mitigation should not rely only on individual awareness. High-stakes systems require procedural safeguards, auditability, second review, uncertainty disclosure, affected-person contestability, and monitoring for unequal error distribution.
