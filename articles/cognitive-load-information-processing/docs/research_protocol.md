# Research Protocol: Cognitive Load and Information Processing

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on cognitive load as the relationship between task demands and limited working-memory capacity. It is intended for cognitive-load experiments, instructional-design studies, multimedia learning studies, medical-education studies, HCI studies, human factors research, decision-support design, and human-AI workflow evaluation.

## Core constructs

### Intrinsic load

Intrinsic load refers to the inherent complexity of the material, especially the number of interacting elements that must be coordinated simultaneously.

### Extraneous load

Extraneous load refers to avoidable mental effort caused by poor design, split attention, redundancy, clutter, unclear sequencing, confusing interface structure, or irrelevant processing demands.

### Germane processing

Germane processing refers to productive effort directed toward schema construction, understanding, integration, transfer, and learning.

### Element interactivity

Element interactivity measures how many pieces of information must be processed together rather than independently.

### Prior knowledge

Prior knowledge measures the learner's existing schema structure or domain familiarity. It can reduce effective load for some learners and generate expertise-reversal effects when instructional supports become redundant for advanced learners.

### Working-memory capacity

Working-memory capacity measures available processing resources for maintaining and manipulating information under task conditions.

### Design quality

Design quality measures how well task information is organized, sequenced, integrated, and made usable.

### Split attention

Split attention occurs when learners must mentally integrate separated sources of information.

### Redundancy

Redundancy occurs when unnecessary duplicate information increases processing burden.

### Subjective effort

Subjective effort measures perceived mental effort, often using rating scales.

### Workload

Workload may include mental demand, temporal demand, effort, frustration, and perceived performance, as in NASA-TLX-style approaches.

### Mental efficiency

Mental efficiency combines performance and effort so that two designs with similar accuracy can be distinguished by the effort required to achieve that accuracy.

## Recommended study designs

1. Worked-example versus conventional problem-solving study.
2. Split-attention versus integrated-materials experiment.
3. Redundancy-reduced versus redundancy-heavy design comparison.
4. Expertise-reversal study comparing novice, intermediate, and expert learners.
5. Multimedia learning experiment comparing modality, signaling, coherence, and contiguity conditions.
6. HCI interface study measuring task success, effort, response time, and errors.
7. Medical or safety simulation measuring load under time pressure.
8. Human-AI decision-support study testing whether automation reduces useful load or creates hidden verification load.
9. Adaptive-learning study comparing fixed and learner-tailored instruction.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not equate difficulty with poor design.
- Separate intrinsic complexity from avoidable extraneous burden.
- Treat productive effort differently from wasted effort.
- Measure performance and effort together where possible.
- Include prior knowledge and expertise as moderators.
- Use repeated measures and participant random effects.
- Include item or task random effects because tasks vary in inherent complexity.
- Avoid vague use of cognitive load as a label for any negative experience.
- Consider accessibility, fatigue, language, disability, stress, institutional pressure, and environmental conditions.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Cognitive load is not distributed evenly across users or learners. Interfaces, workplaces, educational systems, and administrative processes often impose heavier load on people with fewer resources, less prior knowledge, language barriers, disabilities, time pressure, or historically marginalized positions. Researchers should examine whether load is inherent to the task or created by avoidable institutional design.
