# Research Protocol: Cognitive Psychology and Behavioral Economics

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on how cognitive processes shape economic decision making. It is intended for cognitive psychologists, behavioral economists, decision scientists, policy researchers, experimental economists, and applied researchers studying choice under uncertainty.

## Core constructs

### Bounded rationality

Bounded rationality captures the limits of attention, working memory, information access, computation, time, and cognitive effort that shape real-world choice.

### Framing condition

Framing condition records whether the same underlying choice is presented as a gain, loss, risk, opportunity, default, penalty, social norm, or neutral description.

### Reference point

Reference point captures the baseline against which gains and losses are evaluated. Prospect theory predicts that outcomes are often evaluated relative to a reference point rather than final wealth or total utility.

### Loss aversion

Loss aversion measures the degree to which losses are weighted more heavily than comparable gains.

### Default exposure

Default exposure indicates whether a choice architecture included a pre-selected option, opt-in/opt-out condition, or default recommendation.

### Cognitive load

Cognitive load measures the burden placed on attention, working memory, calculation, comparison, or information integration during the choice.

### Intertemporal choice

Intertemporal choice captures trade-offs between immediate and delayed outcomes, including delay discounting and present bias.

### Social influence

Social influence captures peer norms, descriptive norms, reputational signals, authority cues, or collective behavior that changes individual decision making.

## Outcome variables

### Choice

Choice indicates the option selected by the participant or decision unit.

### Risky choice

Risky choice indicates whether the participant selected a probabilistic or uncertain option.

### Default acceptance

Default acceptance indicates whether the participant accepted the default option rather than actively changing it.

### Willingness to pay

Willingness to pay estimates the amount a participant is willing to give up for a good, service, policy, benefit, or risk reduction.

### Decision latency

Decision latency measures time to choose and may reflect difficulty, conflict, attention, calculation, or hesitation.

## Recommended study designs

1. Framing experiment using gain/loss versions of the same choice.
2. Default-effect experiment comparing opt-in and opt-out designs.
3. Intertemporal-choice experiment estimating discounting behavior.
4. Risk-choice experiment estimating probability weighting and loss aversion.
5. Field experiment testing policy nudges.
6. Mixed-methods study combining choice data, interviews, and process measures.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not reduce behavioral economics to irrationality.
- Separate heuristic efficiency from systematic bias.
- Avoid treating nudges as ethically neutral.
- Distinguish individual cognitive limits from structural constraints.
- Preserve heterogeneous effects across income, education, culture, and institutional context.
- Test whether interventions improve welfare or merely increase compliance.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Behavioral interventions can improve decision environments, but they can also manipulate attention, exploit cognitive limits, or shift responsibility onto individuals. Researchers should evaluate transparency, consent, distributional impact, institutional accountability, and whether choice architecture serves public welfare or organizational advantage.
