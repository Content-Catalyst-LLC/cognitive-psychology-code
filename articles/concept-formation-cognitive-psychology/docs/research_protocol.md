# Research Protocol: Concept Formation in Cognitive Psychology

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on concept formation, category learning, abstraction, generalization, feature weighting, prototypes, exemplars, boundary discrimination, and conceptual change. It is intended for cognitive psychologists, learning scientists, developmental researchers, psycholinguists, AI researchers, education researchers, and researchers studying decision making, categorization, expertise, and knowledge representation.

## Core constructs

### Concept

A concept is a mental representation that groups entities, events, properties, or ideas in ways that support categorization, inference, communication, prediction, and action.

### Category accuracy

Category accuracy indicates whether a participant correctly classifies an item into the intended category.

### Prototype distance

Prototype distance measures how far an item is from the central tendency or best representative structure of a category.

### Exemplar similarity

Exemplar similarity measures how strongly a new item resembles stored examples from a category.

### Feature diagnosticity

Feature diagnosticity measures how useful a feature is for distinguishing one category from another.

### Boundary ambiguity

Boundary ambiguity measures how close an item is to a category boundary or how uncertain its category membership is.

### Generalization score

Generalization score measures whether a learned concept is applied appropriately to new cases that differ from training examples.

### Discrimination score

Discrimination score measures whether participants can distinguish similar but conceptually distinct categories.

### Conceptual flexibility

Conceptual flexibility measures whether a participant can shift category use across contexts or revise a concept when new evidence changes the relevant structure.

### Abstraction quality

Abstraction quality measures whether the participant extracts the relevant relational, functional, or feature structure rather than memorizing isolated instances.

## Recommended study designs

1. Prototype-distance categorization experiment.
2. Exemplar-based category-learning experiment.
3. Rule-based versus similarity-based categorization comparison.
4. Category-boundary learning with feedback manipulation.
5. Generalization and transfer study with novel cases.
6. Conceptual-change study comparing pre/post category structure.
7. Human-AI comparison of learned representations, classification boundaries, and generalization behavior.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not equate category membership with typicality.
- Separate memorization of examples from conceptual abstraction.
- Track boundary cases rather than excluding them automatically.
- Model participant and item effects because categories and exemplars vary in familiarity and difficulty.
- Distinguish rule-based, prototype-based, exemplar-based, and theory-based categorization where possible.
- Consider cultural, linguistic, institutional, and educational sources of category structure.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Concepts are not only cognitive tools; they are also social tools. Categories can support learning and communication, but they can also reproduce stereotypes, rigid classifications, deficit framings, and institutional exclusion. Researchers should avoid treating socially produced category systems as neutral or universal without examining their context, power, and consequences.
