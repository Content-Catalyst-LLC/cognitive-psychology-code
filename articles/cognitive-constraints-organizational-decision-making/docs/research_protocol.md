# Research Protocol: Cognitive Constraints in Organizational Decision Making

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on how cognitive constraints affect decision making inside organizations. It is intended for cognitive psychologists, organizational psychologists, decision scientists, behavioral economists, human factors researchers, and applied institutional researchers.

## Core constructs

### Information load

Information load measures the amount, density, ambiguity, and fragmentation of information presented to decision makers. High information load may increase search cost, reduce integration quality, and increase reliance on simplifying heuristics.

### Attention score

Attention score captures the degree to which participants or decision units identify relevant cues, maintain task focus, and distinguish signal from noise.

### Uncertainty level

Uncertainty level captures ambiguity about probabilities, consequences, causal pathways, timelines, and decision reversibility.

### Coordination load

Coordination load measures the burden of aligning multiple actors, departments, data sources, approval chains, or stakeholder interpretations.

### Institutional pressure

Institutional pressure captures political, reputational, incentive, leadership, budgetary, or cultural pressures that may narrow decision frames.

### Psychological safety

Psychological safety measures whether participants or teams can raise concerns, challenge assumptions, or identify errors without fear of punishment.

### Dissent present

Dissent present indicates whether an alternative interpretation, warning, or challenge was available during the decision process.

### Automation reliance

Automation reliance measures the extent to which decision makers depend on dashboards, algorithms, decision-support tools, forecasts, or automated recommendations.

## Outcome variables

### Decision quality

Decision quality can be operationalized as expert-rated quality, objective task performance, alignment with later outcomes, risk recognition accuracy, or distance from a known optimal decision in a simulation.

### Satisficing

Satisficing indicates whether participants selected a good-enough option rather than continuing search, comparison, or deliberation.

### Response time

Response time can index deliberation, difficulty, overload, or hesitation. Interpretation depends on task design.

## Recommended study designs

1. Controlled cognitive experiment with manipulated information load.
2. Vignette-based organizational decision task.
3. Scenario-based strategic decision simulation.
4. Retrospective analysis of institutional decision records.
5. Field study of decision-support system use.
6. Mixed-methods study combining interviews, logs, and quantitative outcomes.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not treat all fast decisions as poor decisions.
- Do not treat all heuristics as biases.
- Separate evidence quality from confidence.
- Distinguish cognitive load from task importance.
- Model team, unit, and participant effects where possible.
- Preserve dissent, uncertainty, and missingness in the research record.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Organizational decision research often involves power, risk, hierarchy, and accountability. Researchers should protect participants who identify institutional failures or unsafe decision processes. When studying marginalized groups, frontline workers, public agencies, or affected communities, the protocol should include safeguards for voice, interpretation, and data governance.
