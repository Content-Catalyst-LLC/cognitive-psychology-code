# Research Protocol: Decision Making in Cognitive Psychology

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on decision making as the cognitive process through which agents represent alternatives, evaluate uncertainty, compare outcomes, accumulate evidence, and select actions under constraint. It is intended for risky-choice experiments, framing studies, decision-time experiments, value-based choice, evidence-accumulation tasks, affect and decision-making studies, naturalistic decision-making research, human factors, institutional decision design, and human-AI decision-support evaluation.

## Core constructs

### Decision condition

Experimental or observational condition, such as control, gain frame, loss frame, time pressure, high cognitive load, low cognitive load, affective cue, AI assistance, or naturalistic decision task.

### Option count

Number of alternatives under consideration. Option count is a useful indicator of choice complexity and search burden.

### Probability

Objective, estimated, or communicated probability associated with an outcome.

### Payoff

Outcome magnitude, monetary or non-monetary, in gain, loss, or mixed domains.

### Expected value

Expected value is the sum of probability-weighted outcomes.

### Expected utility

Expected utility replaces raw outcomes with subjective utility.

### Subjective value

Subjective value allows gains, losses, reference points, affect, and probability weighting to influence the psychological value of an option.

### Reference point

Reference point defines whether an outcome is experienced as a gain or loss.

### Gain/loss frame

Gain/loss frame captures whether formally equivalent information is presented as gain, loss, survival, mortality, success, failure, savings, cost, safety, or harm.

### Loss aversion

Loss aversion measures the asymmetry between psychological response to losses and gains.

### Probability weighting

Probability weighting captures the nonlinear transformation of objective probability into subjective decision weight.

### Evidence strength

Evidence strength measures the quality or diagnosticity of information favoring one option.

### Drift-rate proxy

Drift rate approximates the rate at which evidence accumulates toward a choice.

### Decision threshold

Decision threshold represents the amount of accumulated evidence required before responding.

### Response time

Response time measures the temporal cost of the decision process and can be linked to evidence accumulation, conflict, uncertainty, and threshold setting.

### Confidence

Confidence measures subjective certainty in the chosen option.

### Affective valence

Affective valence records positive or negative emotional appraisal associated with a decision object, outcome, option, or context.

### Cognitive load

Cognitive load records concurrent demands on working memory and attention.

### Time pressure

Time pressure records speed constraints that may change search depth, threshold settings, and reliance on heuristics.

### Uncertainty

Uncertainty measures ambiguity, incomplete information, volatility, or lack of reliable probability estimates.

### Decision quality

Decision quality is a criterion-based evaluation of the decision process or outcome.

### Verification burden

In human-AI decision-support settings, verification burden captures the effort required to audit, contextualize, or correct system output.

## Recommended study designs

1. Gain/loss framing experiment.
2. Risky-choice experiment with probability and payoff manipulation.
3. Expected value versus subjective value comparison.
4. Drift-diffusion-style two-choice response-time task.
5. Time-pressure manipulation.
6. Cognitive-load manipulation.
7. Affect-cue manipulation.
8. Satisficing versus optimizing task.
9. Naturalistic decision-making vignette with uncertainty and feedback.
10. Human-AI decision-support experiment measuring decision quality, trust, verification burden, and automation reliance.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not treat choices as isolated outputs; measure response time, confidence, uncertainty, and information search where possible.
- Separate objective probability from subjective probability.
- Separate payoff magnitude from subjective value.
- Model participant and item effects.
- Consider prior knowledge and domain expertise.
- Include ecological context when studying naturalistic decisions.
- Avoid assuming that longer deliberation always improves decision quality.
- Track feedback quality and delay because decision learning depends on feedback.
- In AI-supported tasks, distinguish assistance from hidden verification burden.
- In institutional contexts, examine unequal consequences of decision error.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Decision systems distribute consequences. A decision model may appear neutral while embedding unequal access, unequal risk, or unequal opportunity to contest an error. High-stakes decision environments should preserve auditability, uncertainty disclosure, second review, transparency, affected-person voice, and monitoring for unequal error distribution.
