# Research Protocol: Risk Perception and Uncertainty

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on risk perception as the cognitive, affective, social, and contextual construction of uncertain outcomes. It is intended for studies of judgment under uncertainty, framing effects, prospect theory, the psychometric paradigm, affective risk judgments, risk communication, public trust, systemic risk, and human-AI decision support.

## Core constructs

### Subjective probability

Subjective probability measures how likely the participant believes an outcome is.

### Objective probability

Objective probability records the stated, modeled, or experimentally assigned probability of an outcome.

### Consequence severity

Consequence severity measures the magnitude of possible harm or loss.

### Affective intensity

Affective intensity measures emotional response to the risk, including fear, dread, concern, worry, alarm, or aversion.

### Perceived controllability

Perceived controllability measures whether the participant believes the risk can be avoided, reduced, or managed through individual or institutional action.

### Familiarity

Familiarity measures how known, ordinary, or personally familiar the risk feels.

### Dread

Dread captures catastrophic potential, involuntariness, fatal consequences, uncertainty, and emotional threat.

### Institutional trust

Trust measures confidence in authorities, experts, institutions, or systems communicating or managing the risk.

### Ambiguity

Ambiguity measures uncertainty about the reliability, completeness, or interpretability of probability information.

### Frame

Frame records whether an outcome is presented as a gain, loss, survival rate, mortality rate, safety improvement, harm reduction, or other communicative framing condition.

### Perceived risk

Perceived risk is a participant-level judgment of danger, concern, or threat under uncertainty.

### Protective action

Protective action indicates whether the participant chooses a risk-reducing behavior.

## Recommended study designs

1. Framing experiment comparing gain and loss descriptions.
2. Affect-heuristic experiment comparing high-affect and low-affect risk descriptions.
3. Psychometric risk-perception study across hazard types.
4. Risk communication experiment comparing numeric, narrative, visual, and uncertainty-explicit messages.
5. Trust manipulation study comparing institutional source credibility.
6. Ambiguity study comparing known probabilities with uncertain probabilities.
7. Prospect-theory task estimating probability weighting and loss aversion.
8. Human-AI risk advisory study measuring calibrated reliance and overconfidence.
9. Public-health, climate, technology, finance, infrastructure, or environmental-risk scenario study.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not treat perceived risk as objective probability.
- Separate probability estimation from consequence severity.
- Measure affect separately from perceived risk.
- Track trust and ambiguity in public-risk communication studies.
- Use repeated trials and participant effects because risk sensitivity varies strongly across individuals.
- Include scenario or item effects because risks differ in familiarity, dread, controllability, and benefit.
- Consider cultural context, institutional trust, prior exposure, marginalization, historical harm, and unequal vulnerability.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Risk is not distributed equally. People may perceive higher risk because they are more exposed, less protected, historically harmed, or less able to trust institutions. Researchers should avoid treating risk perception as irrational fear without examining exposure, vulnerability, power, and past institutional failure.
