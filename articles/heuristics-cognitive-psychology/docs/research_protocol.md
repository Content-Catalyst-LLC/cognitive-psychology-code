# Research Protocol: Heuristics in Cognitive Psychology

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on heuristics as simplified judgment procedures under uncertainty, time pressure, limited attention, limited information, or constrained working memory. It is intended for anchoring studies, availability studies, representativeness and base-rate studies, recognition and fluency experiments, affect-heuristic research, adaptive strategy-selection research, fast-and-frugal heuristics, bounded rationality, HCI decision support, risk perception, medical judgment, legal judgment, financial judgment, public policy, and human-AI interaction.

## Core constructs

### Heuristic type

Heuristic type identifies the dominant shortcut or strategy used in a trial, such as anchoring, availability, representativeness, recognition, fluency, affect, take-the-best, tallying, satisficing, or analytic comparison.

### Anchor value

Anchor value records the initial value or starting point presented to the participant.

### Adjustment

Adjustment measures movement away from the anchor toward the final estimate.

### Recall ease

Recall ease measures how easily examples or instances come to mind.

### Representativeness

Representativeness measures similarity between the case and a category, prototype, stereotype, or familiar pattern.

### Base-rate use

Base-rate use measures whether statistical prior probability influences the judgment.

### Recognition strength

Recognition strength measures whether one option is recognized more easily than another.

### Fluency

Fluency measures speed or subjective ease of processing.

### Affect

Affective valence measures positive or negative feeling attached to an option, hazard, person, organization, or outcome.

### Cue validity

Cue validity measures how predictive a cue is in the environment.

### Cue count

Cue count records how many pieces of information are consulted.

### Information cost

Information cost measures time, effort, attention, or money required to consult additional information.

### Time pressure

Time pressure records deadline constraints or speed demands.

### Cognitive load

Cognitive load measures concurrent working-memory demand.

### Strategy complexity

Strategy complexity estimates the computational complexity of the decision procedure.

### Adaptive fit

Adaptive fit measures whether the heuristic matches the structure of the environment, including cue validity, predictability, redundancy, sample size, and uncertainty.

### Bias magnitude

Bias magnitude measures the gap between heuristic judgment and a criterion such as normative probability, true value, expert judgment, or simulated optimal strategy.

## Recommended study designs

1. Anchoring and adjustment experiment with arbitrary and informative anchors.
2. Availability experiment manipulating recall ease, media salience, or recent exposure.
3. Representativeness and base-rate experiment using category-prototype judgments.
4. Recognition heuristic experiment comparing recognized and unrecognized alternatives.
5. Fluency heuristic experiment manipulating processing ease or exposure.
6. Affect heuristic experiment measuring risk and benefit judgments under emotional cues.
7. Fast-and-frugal tree experiment comparing cue search and classification accuracy.
8. Take-the-best versus weighted-additive comparison under varying cue validity.
9. Satisficing experiment with aspiration thresholds and search costs.
10. Human-AI decision-support experiment measuring whether AI reduces useful search or creates automation bias.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not treat all heuristics as biases.
- Distinguish process evidence from outcome error.
- Measure environmental structure when evaluating adaptive fit.
- Separate retrieval ease from true frequency.
- Separate representativeness from base-rate neglect.
- Separate affect from judged probability where possible.
- Include time pressure, cognitive load, and information cost.
- Model participant and item effects because heuristic use varies across people and tasks.
- Avoid assuming that more information always improves judgment.
- Consider marginalized experience: what looks like a heuristic may reflect real exposure, institutional memory, or learned risk.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Heuristics can protect people from overload, but institutions can also exploit them. Anchoring, default effects, fluency, salience, and affective framing can be used manipulatively in advertising, finance, platform design, legal communication, public policy, and AI systems. Researchers should distinguish supportive decision architecture from coercive or deceptive design.
