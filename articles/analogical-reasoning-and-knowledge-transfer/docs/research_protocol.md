# Research Protocol: Analogical Reasoning and Knowledge Transfer

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on analogical reasoning, structure mapping, source retrieval, relational transfer, and cross-domain knowledge application. It is intended for cognitive psychologists, learning scientists, education researchers, AI researchers, decision scientists, and researchers studying problem solving, expertise, legal reasoning, scientific discovery, and design cognition.

## Core constructs

### Source domain

The source domain is the familiar case, example, story, model, or system from which relational structure is transferred.

### Target domain

The target domain is the new problem, concept, case, or system to which the source structure may be applied.

### Surface similarity

Surface similarity measures shared objects, labels, perceptual features, or topical cues between source and target.

### Structural similarity

Structural similarity measures shared relational organization, causal structure, functional pattern, or higher-order system relations across domains.

### Relational complexity

Relational complexity measures the number and nesting depth of relations that must be represented, aligned, and maintained during analogy.

### Mapping accuracy

Mapping accuracy measures whether elements and relations in the source are aligned with the appropriate corresponding elements and relations in the target.

### Transfer success

Transfer success indicates whether the reasoner applies useful knowledge from the source to solve, explain, or make inferences about the target.

### Inference quality

Inference quality measures the correctness, relevance, and evidential appropriateness of newly projected target-domain claims.

### Schema abstraction

Schema abstraction measures whether the reasoner extracts a general relational pattern that can transfer beyond the immediate source-target pair.

## Recommended study designs

1. Source-target transfer experiment using semantically distant but structurally similar problems.
2. Surface-versus-structural similarity manipulation.
3. Relational-complexity experiment with participant and item random effects.
4. Educational analogy study measuring concept transfer and misconception risk.
5. Decision-making analogy study measuring precedent selection and inference quality.
6. Computational simulation comparing source retrieval and mapping strategies.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not equate surface similarity with analogical strength.
- Separate retrieval success from mapping accuracy.
- Separate mapping accuracy from transfer quality.
- Model problem/item effects because analogical tasks vary substantially in difficulty.
- Track whether a source was noticed, retrieved, mapped, and evaluated.
- Preserve failed analogies, misleading mappings, and overextended inferences.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Analogies are powerful in education, law, policy, science, and public communication. They can clarify unfamiliar systems, but they can also mislead when superficial similarity is mistaken for structural equivalence. Researchers should evaluate analogy quality, boundary conditions, distributional consequences, and whether analogies simplify in ways that distort marginalized or complex realities.
