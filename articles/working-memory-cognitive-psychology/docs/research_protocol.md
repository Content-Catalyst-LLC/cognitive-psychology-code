# Research Protocol: Working Memory in Cognitive Psychology

## Purpose

This protocol supports empirical and simulated research on working memory as a limited-capacity system for temporary maintenance, manipulation, updating, control, and task-guided cognition. It is intended for span tasks, complex span, n-back tasks, serial recall, visuospatial memory, verbal working memory, attentional-control paradigms, cognitive-load studies, learning experiments, interface design studies, and human-AI interaction research.

## Core constructs

### Task type

Task type identifies the paradigm, such as simple span, complex span, operation span, reading span, symmetry span, spatial span, n-back, running span, serial recall, change detection, or dual-task.

### Memory load

Memory load records the number of items, chunks, locations, operations, or relations that must be maintained.

### Serial position

Serial position records item order within a sequence and supports primacy/recency analyses.

### Modality

Modality records whether information is verbal, visual, spatial, auditory, multimodal, or semantic.

### Distractor level

Distractor level measures irrelevant or competing information presented during maintenance.

### Interference

Interference captures proactive, retroactive, semantic, phonological, spatial, or task-switching competition.

### Attentional control

Attentional control measures ability to prioritize relevant information and resist distraction.

### Updating demand

Updating demand measures how much the participant must revise working-memory contents over time.

### Storage demand

Storage demand measures maintenance requirements independent of processing operations.

### Processing demand

Processing demand measures concurrent operations that must be performed while information is maintained.

### Chunking support

Chunking support captures whether the material can be reorganized into meaningful units.

### Rehearsal opportunity

Rehearsal opportunity captures whether participants can refresh or rehearse information.

### Cognitive load

Cognitive load records total processing burden imposed by the task.

### Span score

Span score records the largest sequence length or memory load maintained successfully.

### Updating score

Updating score records performance on tasks requiring replacement, refreshing, or transformation of maintained information.

### Capacity estimate

Capacity estimate approximates the effective number of items or chunks available for active maintenance.

### Overload probability

Overload probability estimates the likelihood that task demand exceeds available working-memory capacity.

### Dual-task cost

Dual-task cost measures performance decline under concurrent processing or distractor load.

## Recommended study designs

1. Simple verbal span task.
2. Operation span or reading span task.
3. Visuospatial span task.
4. N-back updating task.
5. Change-detection capacity task.
6. Serial recall with phonological similarity manipulation.
7. Dual-task maintenance and processing experiment.
8. Cognitive-load learning experiment.
9. Interface-complexity experiment measuring overload and decision quality.
10. Human-AI study measuring whether external support reduces or relocates working-memory burden.

## Measurement cautions

- Do not treat span as a pure storage measure when processing demands are present.
- Separate storage demand from processing demand where possible.
- Record modality, serial position, rehearsal opportunity, and interference.
- Model participant and item effects.
- Trim response times transparently and report sensitivity analyses.
- Distinguish accuracy, response time, confidence, and strategy.
- Avoid interpreting capacity as a fixed container independent of attention, knowledge, and task structure.
- Include accessibility and educational equity considerations when working-memory load affects performance.

## Ethical and institutional considerations

Working-memory limits shape how people navigate schools, workplaces, forms, interfaces, medical instructions, legal procedures, public-benefit systems, and AI tools. Poor design can overload users and then misattribute failure to personal ability. Responsible design should reduce unnecessary load, support chunking, sequence information, preserve context, and make high-stakes tasks cognitively navigable.
